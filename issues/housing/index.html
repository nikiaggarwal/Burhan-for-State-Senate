Policy: Housing

Affordability and Abundance

As Cambridge City Councilor, I helped tackle our housing shortage, bringing down rent prices and creating new homes for thousands of families.

I will pass seven key policies so that every Massachusetts family can afford to put down roots in the city they choose.

The big picture

The Housing Crisis Explained

Addressing the housing crisis has been my life's work.

In this video I dive into how we got here, what can be done, and what it means for us nationally if we can't fix our high housing costs.

I did it in Cambridge. Our multifamily housing zoning made national news because of how transformative it is. We can solve this for the entirety of Massachusetts.

The problem

Why Housing Got This Expensive

For the last 100 years, it has been illegal to build a triple-decker anywhere in Massachusetts.

Immigrants, people of color, and lower-income families moved to the cities in search of a better life. Meanwhile, wealthy communities enacted zoning policies that restricted building denser, cheaper housing to ensure that only the "right" kind of people could live in the neighborhoods they chose.

Cambridge's zoning code literally stated they only wanted millionaires to live there. (Don't worry, I cut that out once I got into office.)

Medford and Somerville's populations were greater in the 1930s than they are today. Cambridge and Winchester's population have only just reached the same levels as their previous peak. By and large, cities' populations have gotten smaller or stayed the same, and no new housing has been built. That means that for children who are born here, if they want to live near their parents, they physically cannot.

It's beyond time to change all that on the state level.

The Austin Benchmark

Austin, Texas was facing a housing crisis ten years ago. New jobs and the city's reputation were drawing in more people, but there wasn't enough housing for everyone. From 2010 to 2019, rents surged by 93%.

Beginning in 2015, Austin instituted new policies to make it easier to build housing. The city changed zoning to allow construction of large apartment buildings. It invested $250 million into affordable housing. And it sped up permitting for those who wanted to build.

The reforms worked. After increasing housing by 30%, Austin's rents started to fall. And they've continued to fall year after year ever since.

The National Picture

On average, the population within blue states is decreasing, while red states are increasing in numbers. Why? Because red states are building housing.

Because of these changes, California is likely to lose four congressional seats by 2030, while Texas is set to gain four. In our political system, people are power.

Massachusetts already lost one congressional seat in 2010. With the Supreme Court allowing partisan gerrymandering and the decreasing political power of blue states, building more housing allows us to regain affordability and reclaim our democracy at the same time.

The plan

How We Will Create More Housing

Idea 01

Uniform zoning

Enact statewide zoning reform. We need one single statewide standard that will make building housing easy and predictable in every municipality. If you can build something in Cambridge, you can build it in Winchester. This doesn't have to be radical — Beacon Hill is already thinking of allowing duplexes everywhere. That's a start. I'll make sure we keep going.

Idea 02

Reform the permitting process

Right now, it takes months to get approvals, and there are multiple hoops to jump through. We should speed up the process and make it less confusing.

Idea 03

Build affordable housing

In Cambridge, I updated the Affordable Housing Overlay, which made it easier to build 100% affordable housing. We need to fund and prioritize affordable housing so that everyone can stay here, regardless of income level.

Idea 04

Single-stair reform

We are losing housing because of outdated fire codes that haven't been proven to make us safer. Larger buildings need to have two stairwells, not one — that takes up square footage that could be someone's home. Right now, we're "studying" this measure. I'll make sure it passes.

Idea 05

Denser housing near transit hubs

Regardless of where you live, we can agree that an apartment building near an MBTA station just makes sense. As State Senator, I'll push the MBTA to develop housing near Alewife and other transit hubs.

Idea 06

Land value tax

Right now, if you do nothing with the land you own, you pay essentially nothing while it increases in value. That leads to empty storefronts and vacant buildings. We should tax the value of the land, not what's on it.

Idea 07

Incentivize Accessibility

The Americans with Disabilities Act doesn’t apply to private housing. Meanwhile, the Fair Housing Act only applies to bigger buildings, so triple deckers and single-family houses don’t have to be accessible. Less than 5% of US housing is usable by someone with moderate mobility difficulties, and less than 1% meets the needs of wheelchair users.

We should close that gap by creating an Accessible Housing Overlay, modeled after my work on Cambridge’s Affordable Housing Overlay. We can use zoning to incentivize the creation of Group 2B (fully accessible) units and even make them permanently affordable. We could grant bonuses for building wheelchair-accessible ramps or installing Deaf-accessible fire alarms. We could ensure housing is built and accessible for all.

The missed opportunity

Alewife Should Already Be Full of Housing

Alewife Station is the perfect place to build denser housing. So why does nobody want to? The MBTA sent out the worst request for proposal I've ever seen. It was vague and unfocused and lacking in vision. When nobody bid on it, the MBTA claimed that market conditions were unfavorable. That's not true.

As State Senator, I'll push the MBTA to develop housing near Alewife and other transit hubs.

Why I can do this

I've spent the last decade winning the fight to build more housing.

I cofounded Abundant Housing Massachusetts and the Welcoming Neighbors Network, the largest national coalition of pro-housing groups. I helped successfully push to get Massachusetts' starter homes initiative on this year's ballot and advocated for the recently passed ROAD to Housing Act.

In Cambridge, I was instrumental in passing the Affordable Housing Overlay. I also ensured we ended exclusionary zoning citywide.

Every step of the way, people told me it couldn't be done. They said it was impossible, but I refused to take no for an answer. And now, Cambridge's multifamily housing zoning made national headlines and our Affordable Housing Overlay is being replicated across the country.

Massachusetts is the first in a lot of things: the most educated state, the best public school system, and best state economy. But we ranked at the very bottom in housing production. It's time for Massachusetts to lead the way.

Let's build

Massachusetts is the 2nd worst state in the nation at creating new housing. It's time we lead the way.
